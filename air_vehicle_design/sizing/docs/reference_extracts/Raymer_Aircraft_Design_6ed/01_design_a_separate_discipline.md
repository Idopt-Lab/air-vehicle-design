## Chapter summary (bullets from chapter-opener page, p. 1)

- Aircraft conceptual design is a specialist skill, and it is more than drafting/CAD or calculation
  and optimization.
- Students should learn it like any other skill, one step at a time.
- The book aims to teach it that way, suited to academia but following industry practice.

## §1.1 What Is Design?

Aircraft design is a discipline of aeronautical engineering distinct from the analytical
disciplines (aerodynamics, structures, controls, propulsion). A designer must know those fields but
spends little time doing that analysis directly, except at the smallest companies — the designer's
job is "design": producing the geometric description of a thing to be built [Raymer, p. 1].

Design looks like drafting from the outside (the product is a drawing), but the real work is
mostly mental. A good design "glides through" later specialist evaluation with few changes needed —
landing gear fits, fuel tanks sit near the c.g., structure is light and simple, aerodynamics is
good, engines install cleanly — not by accident, but from the designer's underlying knowledge and
effort [Raymer, p. 1–2].

Design also depends on the many calculations that decide what to design and how to revise it to
better meet requirements. At a small company one person may do both layout and analysis; at larger
companies, layout is done by the designer while sizing/performance specialists (aided by
aerodynamics, weights, propulsion, stability experts) do the analysis. The book aims to balance and
tie together both design and analysis, matching industry practice [Raymer, p. 2].

### Design: How Does It Start?

Where design "begins" depends on who you ask: the designer says it starts with a new concept; the
sizing specialist says nothing can begin before an initial weight estimate; the customer says it
begins with requirements. Raymer's view: all three are correct — design begins with all of them
simultaneously, at low sophistication, then iterates toward a solution as more sophisticated methods
get applied [Raymer, p. 2].

This iteration is the "Design Wheel" (Fig. 1.1): a concept is drawn to meet initially-imagined
requirements from initial sizing; the concept is analyzed (aerodynamics, performance); results feed
sizing trade studies/optimization; that drives design changes and can even change the requirements
themselves, chasing a more cost-effective answer to the customer's real need. The concept is revised
and the wheel turns again, and again, until a well-balanced design emerges alongside a
well-balanced set of requirements — "you 'design' the requirements as you are designing the
aircraft" [Raymer, p. 2].

Key lesson for students: design is iterative. You do not draw one neat initial concept (the
"Dash-One"), analyze it, and stop — you learn from it, apply judgment and computational
optimization, find what to improve, and redraw it again, and maybe again [Raymer, p. 2].

### How a Design Project Actually Begins

Real projects begin in about as many ways as there are designs. Homebuilders start from a dream and
a wish to fly something self-designed, caring less about exact performance/range/payload than about
the experience of having designed it themselves [Raymer, p. 3].

Most projects nominally start from stated requirements, but those begin fuzzy — "win the war" for a
military design, "make money" for a civilian one. Reaching a "clean" requirement set takes a long,
iterative process best run in parallel with a design layout built by experts, which grounds the
requirements in reality even if that particular design is never built [Raymer, p. 3].

New projects usually originate inside the aircraft companies rather than the customer community.
Airlines do not walk into Boeing or Airbus with a spec for a new airliner; instead the manufacturers
continuously scout market needs and, on finding one, design an airplane and pitch it to airlines
[Raymer, p. 3].

On the military side, service design offices study future needs but rarely finalize detailed
requirements without close work with industry. More often, per Raymer's own experience, a company
develops an initial idea and design studies and takes them to the potential customer; the final
requirements for a major program do come from the military customer, but only after a great deal of
community-wide work [Raymer, p. 3–4].

A common misconception among people outside the field is that an "outsider" can invent a design
concept and sell it directly to a customer, as if a good mousetrap sells itself. In practice, even
a promising outside concept gets independently re-studied by the company or military office, which
then develops its own requirements (possibly revised) and either designs its own concept or issues
an RFP for a design-study contract — the odds of an outsider's unsolicited proposal beating a major
aircraft company's are minuscule, small-business set-asides included. This does not mean outsiders
have no path: they must develop a design concept against an actual customer RFP and prove they can
design, build, fly, produce, and support it [Raymer, p. 4].

Not every airplane is designed for military or commercial utility. Some of the most famous
airplanes, starting with the very first, were built for concept validation and experimental
research ("X" planes) — range and performance matter only insofar as they support the research
goal. Early effort on such projects centers on trading program cost against how fully the new
concept/technology can be "exercised": too cheap a design proves little, too expensive a design may
never fly. The X-31 is cited as a classic example of this tradeoff, with the author leading months
of cost-vs-capability trade studies before the concept was validated [Raymer, p. 4]. And some
airplanes, finally, are designed purely for fun [Raymer, p. 4].

### An Airplane Designer: How Can I Become One?

Many aeronautical-engineering students dream of becoming a conceptual designer; few do — advanced
design departments at major companies are small, and smaller companies often lack a dedicated new-
aircraft team, so entry-level (or any) jobs in the field are scarce [Raymer, p. 4–5].

Practical suggestions Raymer gives for pursuing it anyway: learn to fly (or at least pass the FAA
Private Pilot written exam); fly model airplanes, especially original designs; learn welding,
machine tools, and composite fabrication; get good at CAD. Attend a strong engineering university
with a design-oriented curriculum and design-minded professors; take a broad range of aero subjects
(aerodynamics, structures, controls, propulsion); pursue independent-study design projects and
wind-tunnel experience; enter (and try to win) the AIAA student design competition. Seek a summer
internship or work-study position at a major aerospace company, a government agency (e.g. NASA), or
a contracting engineering firm — interns are often later offered real jobs, sometimes at higher pay.
Finally, take enough coursework in a technical specialty as a fallback, so a first job is available
even outside design proper, with the option to build a reputation internally and later transfer into
design [Raymer, p. 5].

Raymer also points to his own non-technical book, *Living in the Future — The Education and
Adventures of an Advanced Aircraft Designer*, part biography and part insider history of projects
he worked on (X-31 through BQM-177, plus early involvement in programs that became the F-22, B-2,
F-35, and T-45). Two reviewer quotes are given: Peter Garrison (*Flying Magazine*, Nov. 2010)
calling it "a big buffet of all kinds of stuff... a close-up of the human lives inside the gigantic
and impersonal-seeming Rockwells and Lockheeds of this world"; and Darold Cummings (chief
configuration designer, Northrop YF-23), contrasting it as covering the "pathos" of being an
aircraft designer to the textbook's "ethos" [Raymer, p. 5].

## §1.2 The Book: What Is Here and How It Is Organized

The textbook describes the process for developing a credible conceptual design from a set of
requirements, deliberately following the sequence of a "real" design project at a major aerospace
company. The first half covers requirements definition, sizing, and preliminary geometry through
configuration layout (requirements, aerodynamics, structures, propulsion, subsystems); the second
half covers concept analysis and optimization, emphasizing how the design improves on the next
iteration [Raymer, p. 6].

The book has 24 chapters, organized as follows [Raymer, p. 6–7]:
- **Ch. 2–3:** overall introduction to the design process. Ch. 2 covers how conceptual design fits
  into overall aircraft development; Ch. 3 is a "first-pass" design procedure (layout, analysis,
  takeoff-weight estimation, trade studies) to introduce the essential concepts.
- **Ch. 4–11:** developing the initial configuration layout — conceptual sketch, initial sizing,
  wing geometry selection, lofting, inboard layout, propulsion integration, crew station,
  payload/passenger compartment, fuel system, landing gear — plus observability, producibility, and
  supportability considerations. (Terminology sometimes assumes a drafting table, but the same
  techniques apply on a modern CAD system.)
- **Ch. 12–18:** detailed analysis of the resulting layout — aerodynamics, weights, installed
  propulsion characteristics, stability and control, performance, cost, and sizing. Methods are
  simplified enough for a student to work through the whole process in one course. No textbook can
  match industry's (largely proprietary, highly computerized) methods, but the book's methods give
  reasonable results for most aircraft categories — good enough, per Raymer, to sanity-check
  sophisticated computer results; large disagreement more often means the computer result is wrong.
- **Ch. 19:** optimization methods — classic carpet plots, parametric trade studies, and modern
  schemes (decomposition, response surfaces, genetic algorithms).
- **Ch. 20–23:** vehicles that differ from "normal" aircraft. Ch. 20 (new to this edition) covers
  electric aircraft (motors, controllers, power supplies, analysis); Ch. 21 covers vertical flight
  (helicopters, VTO jets); Ch. 22 covers flight-regime extremes (very slow to very fast: spacecraft,
  hypersonics, airships); Ch. 23 covers unconventional configurations (flying wings, canard pushers,
  joined wings, asymmetric aircraft) — building on conventional-aircraft methods with added
  considerations.
- **Ch. 24:** two complete worked design-project examples, used (instead of scattered example
  calculations throughout the text) to show how all the pieces fit together as a whole.

As an AIAA Education Series title, the book targets college students and is written to be as
self-contained as possible. Industry designers can ask a functional specialist for a reasonable
first-guess tire size, inlet capture area, or composite weight savings; universities have no such
specialists on hand, so the book supplies empirical methods and rule-of-thumb approximations for
initial parameter estimates [Raymer, p. 7].

The appendices carry supporting reference material: unit conversions, atmosphere tables, airfoil
and engine data, and a summary of current civil/military design requirements drawn mainly from FARs
and Mil-Specs [Raymer, p. 7].

## What We've Learned (chapter close, p. 7)

Aircraft conceptual design is a separate discipline with its own history, methods, and rules of
thumb — a skill built through education and experience. Doing it well yields a better airplane with
more capability at lower cost.
