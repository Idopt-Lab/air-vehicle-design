# Chapter 1 — Introduction

**Source:** Roskam, *Airplane Design, Part II: Preliminary Configuration Design and Integration of the Propulsion System* (DARcorporation), Chapter 1 "Introduction," printed pp. 1–6.

Short scope-setting chapter. It states the purpose of the eight-part series, lists the
eleven configuration-design decisions, and defines the two preliminary-design sequences
and the Class I / Class II method split. One figure (Fig. 1.1) carries numeric data and
is reproduced in full below. No numbered equations.

## 1. Purpose and organization of the series

The series teaches design methodology and design decision making to aerospace
engineering students. The eight parts are:

| Part | Title |
|---|---|
| I | Preliminary Sizing of Airplanes |
| II | Preliminary Configuration Design and Integration of the Propulsion System |
| III | Layout Design of Cockpit, Fuselage, Wing and Empennage: Cutaways and Inboard Profiles |
| IV | Layout Design of Landing Gear and Systems |
| V | Component Weight Estimation |
| VI | Preliminary Calculation of Aerodynamic, Thrust and Power Characteristics |
| VII | Determination of Stability, Control and Performance Characteristics: FAR and Military Requirements |
| VIII | Airplane Cost Estimation: Design, Development, Manufacturing and Operating |

Part II gives a systematic approach to configuration design, including integration of the
propulsion system (p. 1).

## 2. The eleven configuration-design decisions

Configuration design is the set of the following decisions (pp. 1–3). Roskam states
explicitly that decisions 1–11 are **not** in an order of importance (p. 3).

**1. Selection of the overall configuration**
- Conventional (that means tail aft)
- Flying wing (that means no horizontal tail and no canard)
- Tandem wing
- Canard
- Three Surface
- Joined Wing

**2. Selection of the fuselage layout**
- Arrangement of crew, passengers, baggage, fuel, cargo and other payloads
- Cockpit or flightdeck layout
- Cabin layout
- Window, door and emergency exit layout
- Check of fuel, baggage and cargo volume
- Weapons and stores arrangement
- Access for loading and unloading
- Access for maintenance and for servicing

For a flying wing design, all of these items must be arranged to fit in the wing.

**3. Selection of propulsion system type(s)**
- Piston/propeller with or without supercharging
- Turbo/propeller or prop-fan
- Propfan or unducted fan
- Turbojet or turbofan
- Ramjet or rocket
- Rotary/diesel
- Electric (solar, microwave, lithium fuel cell)

**4. Selection of the number of engines and/or propellers**

**5. Integration of the propulsion system**
- Propellers: pusher or tractor
- Engines buried in the fuselage or in the wing
- Engines in nacelles on the fuselage or on the wing
- Disposition of engines and nacelles

**6. Selection of planform design parameters for the wing and for the empennage (tails and/or canard)**
- Size (i.e. area) of wing
- Aspect ratio
- Sweep angle (fixed or variable)
- Thickness ratio
- Airfoil type
- Taper ratio
- Control surface size and disposition
- Incidence angle (fixed or variable)
- Dihedral angle

**7. Selection of type, size and disposition of high lift devices**
- Mechanical or powered (blown) flaps
- Trailing edge and/or leading edge devices

**8. Selection of landing gear type and disposition**
- Fixed or retractable
- Tail dragger, tricycle or tandem
- Number of struts and tires
- Wheel location up and down
- Feasibility of gear retraction

**9. Selection of major systems to be employed by the airplane**
- Flight control system, primary and secondary
- Auxiliary power unit (APU)
- Fuel system
- Hydraulic system
- Pneumatic system
- Electrical system
- Oxygen system
- Environmental control system (this includes the cabin pressurization system)
- Anti-icing and de-icing system
- Spray system (i.e. for agricultural airplanes)
- Navigation and guidance system
- Fire control system

**10. Selection of structural arrangement, type of structure and manufacturing breakdown**
- Metallic, composite or mixture
- Arrangement of primary structure of major airplane components
- Attachment structure for landing gear
- Manufacturing and assembly sequence

**11. Determination of the cost of research, development, manufacturing and operation**
- Assessment of profit potential (civil)
- Assessment of mission effectiveness = (availability) x (survivability) x (accuracy), for military airplanes
- Assessment of life cycle cost (civil and military)

## 3. Important notes

**Note 1 (p. 3):** Configuration design is a non-unique and iterative process.

**Note 2 (p. 4):** During the early phases of configuration design, 90 percent of the life
cycle cost of an airplane gets locked in.

Many different methods can give a satisfactory design. More than one configuration, and
sometimes a very different configuration, can satisfy the same mission specification. The
Boeing B-47 and the AVRO Vulcan jet bombers are the classic example: very different
configurations, very similar mission specifications (p. 4).

## 4. The two preliminary design sequences

Chapter 2 divides configuration design into two preliminary design (p.d.) sequences
(p. 4):

1. **p.d. sequence I** — 16 design steps. Its objective is to decide if a given
   configuration is feasible, with the minimum amount of engineering work.
2. **p.d. sequence II** — 30 design steps. Its objective is a reasonably detailed layout
   of a given configuration, so that its mission capability can be compared with
   competing concepts with confidence.

In each sequence you must estimate drag, stability and control, weight and balance, and
the other factors that the 11 decisions need. The depth of the estimate must agree with
the depth of the sequence.

**Class I methods** are the engineering methods used with p.d. sequence I. They have
limited accuracy but need only a small number of engineering manhours. Part II uses Class
I methods only.

**Class II methods** are the engineering methods used with p.d. sequence II. They have
fairly good accuracy but need a large expenditure of engineering manhours. Parts III
through VIII deal with Class II methods (pp. 4–6).

## Figure 1.1 — Example of Radically Different Configurations with Similar Mission Performance
*[Roskam Part II, Fig. 1.1, p. 5]*

Threeview drawings of the Boeing B-47H and the AVRO Vulcan B2 (drawings only, no plotted
data), with the tabulated comparison below. The table is reproduced exactly as printed.

| Parameter | Units | B-47H | Vulcan B2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| W_TO | lbs | 202,000 | 200,000 |
| S | ft² | 1,400 | 3,964 |
| S_wet | ft² | 11,300 | 9,600 |
| b | ft | 116 | 111 |
| (W/S)_TO | psf | 144 | 50.5 |
| (W/b)_TO | lbs/ft | 1,741 | 1,801 |
| A | – | 9.6 | 3.1 |
| C_f (assumed) | – | 0.0030 | 0.0030 |
| f | ft² | 34.0 | 29.0 |
| dC_D/dC_L² (e = 0.8 assumed) | – | 0.041 | 0.128 |
| (L/D)_max | – | 15.8 | 16.4 |
| C_L at (L/D)_max | – | 0.77 | 0.24 |

**Symbols:** W_TO = takeoff weight; S = wing reference area; S_wet = wetted area;
b = wing span; A = aspect ratio; C_f = equivalent skin friction coefficient;
f = equivalent parasite area, f = C_f · S_wet; dC_D/dC_L² = induced drag factor
= 1/(π A e); e = Oswald efficiency factor.

**Consistency check (made by this extract, not printed in the book):** each tabulated
value reproduces from the others with the standard relations. f = C_f·S_wet gives 33.9
and 28.8 (printed 34.0 and 29.0). 1/(πAe) gives 0.0414 and 0.128. With C_D0 = f/S,
(L/D)_max = 0.5/sqrt(C_D0 · dC_D/dC_L²) gives 15.8 and 16.3, and
C_L at (L/D)_max = sqrt(C_D0 / (dC_D/dC_L²)) gives 0.77 and 0.24. No misprint found.
