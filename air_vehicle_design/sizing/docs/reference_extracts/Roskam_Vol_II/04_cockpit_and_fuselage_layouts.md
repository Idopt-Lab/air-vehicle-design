# Chapter 4 — Design of Cockpit and Fuselage Layouts

**Source:** J. Roskam, *Airplane Design, Part II: Preliminary Configuration Design and Integration of the Propulsion System*, Chapter 4 "Design of Cockpit and Fuselage Layouts," printed pp. 107–122 (PDF indices 118–133).

This chapter is a step-by-step layout procedure. It has no numbered equations. Its
citable data are one geometry table (Table 4.1), a set of structural-depth allowances,
and the dimensioned example layouts (cabin cross sections, seat dimensions, fuselage
general arrangements). Sections 4.2.1–4.2.3 are worked examples for three design
studies: Selene (twin propeller), Ourania (jet transport) and Eris (fighter).

---

## 4.1 A Procedure for the Design of Cockpit and Fuselage Layouts

The chapter is Step 4 of preliminary-design sequence I of Chapter 2. The procedure
gives cockpit and fuselage layouts that satisfy the crew, passenger and payload parts of
the mission specification. For military airplanes it also covers the weapons and stores
layout on the fuselage.

### Step 4.1 — List the items to be located in the fuselage

Read the mission specification. Make a list of the crew, payload and operational items
that must go in the fuselage. The step assumes the configuration is not a flying wing.
Typical list items (p. 107):

1. Number and weight of cockpit crew members
2. Number and weight of cabin crew members
3. Number and weight of "special duty" crew members (radar and systems operators)
4. Number and weight of passengers
5. Weight and volume of carry-on baggage
6. Weight and volume of check-in baggage
7. Weight and volume of cargo
8. Number, weight and size of cargo containers
9. Weight and volume of special operational equipment (sensor and computer equipment
   for patrol airplanes)
10. Weight and volume of military payload (guns, stores, bombs, torpedoes, missiles)
11. Weight and volume of fuel carried in the fuselage
12. Radar equipment
13. Auxiliary power unit (APU)
14. Beaching requirements (flying boats)

### Step 4.2 — Make a dimensioned drawing of the cabin interior

Decide the size and shape of the fuselage cross section, the position of the cabin floor
in that cross section, and the volume necessary for items 1–14. Define the access doors,
hatches and emergency exits. The certification base sets minimum size, position and
number of exits (data in Part III, Ref. 2).

For passenger/troop transports and business airplanes, decide (p. 108):

1. Number of persons abreast
2. Number and size of aisles
3. Type of seating: first class, business class, tourist class or economy class
4. Cabin provisions: closets, toilets, overhead storage compartments, galleys
5. Seating provisions for the cabin crew

For cargo airplanes, loading and off-loading from both ends can be a requirement. Large
doors and ramps then control the fuselage structural layout. For military airplanes,
allow for guns, ammunition containers, missiles and other weapons.

### Step 4.3 — Add the structural depth

Add distances to the cabin interior layout for the depth of the fuselage frames,
bulkheads and skins. Typical allowances (p. 109):

| Airplane class | Structural depth allowance |
|---|---|
| Small commercial airplanes | 1.5 in |
| Fighters and trainers | 2 in |
| Large transports | 0.02·d_f + 1 in |

where `d_f` = fuselage diameter (same units as the result, i.e. inches).

### Step 4.4 — Finish the exterior lines of the cabin part of the fuselage

### Step 4.5 — Make a dimensioned drawing of the cockpit

Lay out the cockpit for the necessary pilot visibility and for pilot reach to the
essential controls (detail data in Part III). Fair the cockpit exterior into the
fuselage exterior with the least possible added drag.

### Step 4.6 — Make a dimensioned drawing of the complete fuselage, including the rear cone

Figure 4.1 defines the fuselage geometry parameters. Table 4.1 gives the ranges in
current use. Do not go outside these ranges without a good reason.

The fuselage cone is a smooth transition from the maximum cross section to the end of
the fuselage. If the fineness ratio of the cone is too low, base drag is large, but
fuselage weight can decrease. If it is too high, friction drag and weight are both
large. A long cone increases the tail moment arm and so decreases the necessary tail
area, and the opposite is also true. The choice is a trade-off.

- **Caution 1.** The cone geometry can limit take-off rotation about the rear gear. Make
  sure the cone does not prevent rotation.
- **Caution 2.** Twin-boom configurations tend to give a fuselage with a small fineness
  ratio. Examples: AW Argosy (Ref. 14, Section 14.2) and Fairchild C-119 (Ref. 29,
  Section 14.2). Both had high drag because of the fuselage configuration. Trade the
  drag decrease of a larger fineness ratio against the weight increase.
- **Caution 3.** For flying boats the lower fuselage (hull) must have the correct
  hydrodynamic lines. See Part III for hull shapes.

### Step 4.7 — Document the decisions

Write a brief descriptive report of Steps 4.1–4.6 with clear dimensioned drawings. One
drawing must be an inboard profile.

## 4.2 Example Applications

Three examples, all worked through the Step 4.1 – Step 4.7 sequence:

- 4.2.1 Twin engine propeller driven airplane: **Selene**
- 4.2.2 Jet transport: **Ourania**
- 4.2.3 Fighter: **Eris**

### 4.2.1 Twin Engine Propeller Driven Airplane (Selene)

**Step 4.1.** Table 2.17 of Part I gives the mission. Fuselage items: six passengers
(this includes the pilot); 200 lb of luggage. The mission specification does not give a
baggage volume; comparison with competitors (Ref. 8) shows 40 ft³ is acceptable.

**Step 4.2.** Two-abreast layout: two cockpit seats and four cabin seats, three rows.
Most airplanes of this class have a flat-sided cross section, which is acceptable for an
unpressurized fuselage and easy to make. A circular section is best for a pressurized
fuselage but becomes bulky for this size. Because future versions of the Selene will be
pressurized, the double-circle section of Figure 4.2a was chosen, with slab sides that
join the two circles. The cabin access door is directly behind the pilot on the left
side, because the pusher propellers are too close to a rear door.

Comparison of internal cabin dimensions (p. 112):

| Airplane Type | Length (ft) | Max. Width (ft) | Max. Height (ft) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beech Duke | 11.8 | 4.2 | 4.3 |
| Beech Baron M58 | 12.6 | 3.5 | 4.2 |
| Cessna T303 | 13.6 | 4.0 | 4.0 |
| Piper PA-44-180 | 8.0 | 3.5 | 4.0 |
| **Selene** | **18.5** | **4.25** | **4.4** |

**Step 4.3.** Figure 4.2a shows the structural depth of 1.5 in (consistent with §4.1
Step 4.3 for small commercial airplanes).

**Steps 4.4 and 4.5.** Figure 4.2b gives the cockpit arrangement and the fairing into the
cabin exterior lines. Over-the-nose visibility is 15 deg.

**Step 4.6.** Figure 4.2b gives the rear fuselage cone. The up-slope of the bottom of the
cone is 15 deg, which agrees with attached flow and with take-off rotation. Cone fineness
ratio = 2.7. Overall fuselage fineness ratio = 7.2. Both agree with Table 4.1 (Twins).

### 4.2.2 Jet Transport (Ourania)

**Step 4.1.** Table 2.18 of Part I gives the mission. Fuselage items (p. 114):

1. 150 passengers
2. 150 × 30 = 4,500 lb of luggage
3. Flight deck crew of two plus three cabin attendants
4. 5 × 30 = 150 lb of luggage for the crew

There is no requirement for cargo containers. Assume the total 4,650 lb of luggage goes
in containers below the cabin floor. Typical luggage density = **12.5 lb/ft³**, which
gives a requirement of **372 ft³** of baggage volume. Because many 727s and 737s are in
service, container interchangeability with them is desirable. A typical Boeing 727 belly
container holds about **80 ft³**, so five containers are necessary.

**Step 4.2.** Comparable airplanes (Ref. 8) have five- or six-abreast seating. A circular
cross section is necessary to keep the pressurized shell weight down. Five abreast gives
30 seat rows; six abreast gives 25 seat rows. Five abreast would make any growth version
very long, so six abreast was chosen. Aisle width = **22 in**. Seat pitch = **34 in**
(Part III). The circle is drawn so the aisle height is reasonable and the shoulders of
window-seat passengers do not touch the side wall.

Comparison of cabin interior dimensions (p. 115):

| Airplane Type | Seats Abreast | Length (ft) | Max. Width (ft) | Max. Height (ft) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boeing 737-300 | 6 | 68.5 | 11.5 | 7.2 |
| McDD DC9-80 | 5 | 101 | 10.1 | 6.8 |
| Airbus 320 | 6 | NA | 12.2 | 7.3 |
| **Ourania** | **6** | **76.6** | **12.4** | **7.5** |

**Step 4.3.** Figure 4.4 shows the exterior cross section. Structural depth = **4.7 in**,
consistent with §4.1 Step 4.3 (0.02·d_f + 1 in).

**Step 4.4.** Figure 4.5 shows the exterior lines of the cabin.

**Step 4.5.** Figure 4.6 gives the flight deck, with added seats for check pilots.
Visibility looks acceptable; a visibility pattern drawing (method in Part III) is
necessary to confirm it.

**Step 4.6.** Figure 4.7 gives the complete fuselage. Rear cone fineness ratio = **3.5**;
whole fuselage fineness ratio = **10.1**. Both agree with Table 4.1 (Jet Transports).

**Step 4.7.** Omitted in the book to save space.

### 4.2.3 Fighter (Eris)

**Step 4.1.** Table 2.19 of Part I gives the mission. Fuselage items:

1. Pilot with ejection seat
2. GAU 8/A multi-barrel cannon
3. Two engines

Item 3 comes from the configuration choice of sub-section 3.5.3, not from the mission
specification.

**Steps 4.2 – 4.7 (taken together).** A fighter must be packed tightly to save weight,
wetted area and volume, so the steps are not separable. Figure 3.61 (DeHavilland DH110
Sea Vixen) is the guide configuration. Decisions made:

- The large ammunition container goes behind the pilot, to keep the fuselage short.
- The cannon goes forward of and below the pilot.
- The nose gear retracts forward into the nose. Cannon and nose gear compete for the same
  space, so they are separated laterally: cannon on the right side, nose gear on the left.
- The engines are as close behind the ammunition container as possible.
- The wing torque box passes above the ammunition container, to keep fuselage depth small.
  This gives a high wing, which forces "armpit" type inlet ducts.
- Cannon exhaust gas can enter the inlets, so a gas deflector strake is installed on the
  nose.
- Downward visibility over the nose is 15 deg (Figure 4.9).

The mission specification also calls for twenty 500 lb bombs carried externally:
**8 bombs** mounted conformally under the fuselage and **12 bombs** on racks under the
wings at the same spanwise station that carries the tail booms.
