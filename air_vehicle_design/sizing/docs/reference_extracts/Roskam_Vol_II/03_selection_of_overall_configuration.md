## Chapter 3 introduction [pp. 25-27]

Configuration design is driven by marketing, emotion, and styling in addition to technical
matters. This chapter discusses the technical matters only [p. 25].

Seven technical considerations control the choice of the overall configuration [pp. 25-26]:

1. **Put the fuel c.g., the payload c.g., and the empty-weight c.g. at the same longitudinal
   station.** This limits c.g. travel. Limited c.g. travel needs less trim control power, so
   the airplane has less wetted area. This rule has a major influence on where you put the
   components that most affect overall c.g. location.

2. **Select the wing critical Mach number so the airplane does not cruise far into the drag
   rise.** Wing sweep angle, airfoil type, and airfoil thickness ratio must all be chosen to
   prevent too much drag rise at the cruise Mach number.

3. **Keep the wing critical Mach number below the critical Mach number of the stabilizing and
   control surfaces.** Therefore choose the thickness ratio, sweep angle, and aspect ratio of
   the tail surfaces to give a critical Mach number higher than that of the wing.

4. **Integrate the major components (nacelle-on-wing, nacelle-on-fuselage, wing-on-fuselage)
   so interference drag is a minimum.** Ideally, connecting or intersecting items meet as
   close as possible to 90 degrees. If that is not possible, large fairings are necessary to
   prevent interference drag penalties. At high subsonic speed, local area ruling is often
   necessary to decrease subsonic wave drag (see Part VI, Ref. 5).

5. **For fighters with a supersonic cruise or supersonic maneuver requirement, wave drag is an
   essential design consideration.** To make supersonic wave drag a minimum, arrange the
   configuration so the cross-sectional area distribution (taken as an equivalent body of
   revolution) is smooth. Ideally this distribution approximates the Sears-Haack shape.
   Supersonic area ruling is discussed in Part VI (Ref. 5).

6. **Arrange major intersecting structural components to prevent duplicated heavy structure.**
   Low structural weight comes only from combining several functions into one major structural
   element. Roskam calls this *structural synergism*. Example: in a high-wing transport with
   fuselage-mounted main gear, attach the landing gear to the same fuselage frames that carry
   the wing.

7. **When you decide where the major components go:**
   THINK LIGHT, THINK SIMPLE, THINK ACCESSIBILITY, THINK MAINTAINABILITY AND THINK COST.
   Above a certain cost level no airplane will sell.

Configurations are often an outgrowth of an existing configuration, and this is common in the
large companies. The Boeing 707, 727, 737, and 757 all use the same fuselage cross section.
The DC-9 and the MD-80/-82 series do the same [p. 26].

A new configuration is usually the result of many trade studies by different teams, each
looking for the most economical answer to one mission requirement. Two or more teams in one
company may work the same mission with different configuration approaches. Companies would
not do this if a configuration could be selected on a direct rational basis [p. 27].

There is no straightforward, unique procedure that selects the configuration that best
satisfies the customer requirements. Too many variables are involved, and most of them defy
mathematical modelling [p. 27].

**CAUTION printed by the author** [p. 28]. Keep four points in mind when you read the pros and
cons in this section:

1. In airplane configuration design there are **no** absolute pros and cons, **only** relative
   pros and cons.
2. During configuration design many pros and cons are traded against each other, and a
   compromise is struck.
3. Unless you took part in the decision process that produced a given configuration, it is
   very hard to know how the pros and cons were compromised.
4. It is almost impossible to discuss pros and cons without some personal bias. Reader beware.

### §3.1.1 Homebuilts [p. 29]

Figures 3.1-3.3 show twelve typical homebuilt configurations. Observations [p. 29]:

1. The airplanes range from simple, basic, low-performance machines (Sizer Sapphire, Fig.
   3.2b) to sophisticated high-performance machines (Sequoia Model 300, Fig. 3.1c).
2. All the homebuilts shown are conventional, except the tandem-wing Piel C.P.500 (Fig. 3.3d).
3. Some homebuilders must store the airplane at home. This sometimes leads to a wing-fold
   mechanism. Examples: Figs. 3.1a, 3.2a, and 3.3a.
4. Homebuilders show no preference between tricycle and tail-dragging landing gear. Both are
   widely used. But note the preference for **fixed** landing gear, for cost and simplicity.
5. The homebuilts shown use tractor, piston-propeller propulsion. The Coot (Fig. 3.3a) is the
   only exception, and it is also the only amphibious layout shown.
6. All wings are cantilever, except those of Figs. 3.2b, 3.2d, and 3.3d.
7. Wing planforms vary widely, from bi-plane to mono-plane and from straight untapered to
   elliptical. Cost and build hours matter to the homebuilder. An efficient elliptical wing
   costs the builder the time it takes to build it.
8. Note the preference for low-wing designs. The probable cause is the wish to attach the
   landing gear to the wing and to keep the gear short, and therefore light.

### §3.1.2 Single engine propeller driven airplanes [p. 33]

Figures 3.4-3.6 show twelve typical configurations. Observations [p. 33]:

1. Nine of these airplanes have low wings, three have high wings. All use cantilever wings
   except the Cessnas of Fig. 3.5, which have externally braced wings.
2. All configurations are tractor, except the Poschel P-300 Equator of Fig. 3.5.
3. Vertical placement of the horizontal tail: three have T-tails, nine have the horizontal
   tail roughly at the root of the vertical tail. T-tails in this category share two problems:
   - a) The T-tail is high, so it is difficult to inspect without a ladder.
   - b) The horizontal tail is out of the propeller slipstream, so rotation during take-off is
     more difficult, and the take-off run is longer. The Poschel P-300 avoids this by putting
     the propeller at the T-tail junction.
4. Longitudinal placement of the horizontal tail: on several airplanes the horizontal tail is
   aft of the rudder hinge line. This keeps the rudder out of the separated horizontal-tail
   wake after a stall or on entry to a spin, which makes spin recovery easier. It does not
   mean the other types cannot recover from a spin.
5. Only four of these airplanes have retractable landing gear. A retractable gear decreases
   cruise drag but increases both acquisition cost and maintenance cost. In this type of
   airplane it also tempts the pilot to forget to lower the gear before landing.
6. All but one have swept-aft vertical tails. At low sweep angle this improves the product of
   tail moment arm and tail lift-curve slope, and therefore improves vertical tail
   effectiveness. Vertical tails are also swept for styling.

### §3.1.3 Twin engine propeller driven airplanes [pp. 37-38]

Figures 3.7-3.9 show twelve examples. Observations [pp. 37-38]:

1. Part I (Ref. 1) lists some of these airplanes as regional propeller driven airplanes. Any
   categorization of airplanes is arbitrary. At the high-weight end of the twins and the
   low-weight end of the regionals there is much overlap.
2. Four of the twins are high-wing airplanes. The other eight have low wings.
3. The only pure pusher is the Piaggio P166. The Cessna 336 is a pusher-tractor combination,
   also called centerline thrust. This type is much easier to control with one engine out.
   Engine-out control problems are a major design consideration in conventional twins.
4. Several twins have the horizontal tail directly in the propeller slipstream. This makes
   controllability a function of engine power. There can be advantages. But for twins with
   low power loadings (very powerful engines) the slipstream can cause large tail fatigue
   problems.
5. Also weigh controllability in a go-around. In a low-power approach, with the airplane
   trimmed for that condition, sudden application of power can give a large increase in
   control force. A physically weak pilot can then have longitudinal control problems. Keeping
   the tail out of the slipstream decreases this problem. An interim solution is to give the
   horizontal tail enough geometric dihedral.
6. Nine of the twins have retractable landing gear. Most twins retract the gear into the
   wings. This is not the lightest solution, but weight and balance sometimes leave no choice.
7. All twins have single-wheel main gears, except the Islander of Fig. 3.9a.
8. Several twins have sharp-edged dorsal fins. These increase directional stability and remove
   rudder-lock.
9. Nacelle/wing integration methods differ widely. There is no agreement on the nacelle/wing
   shape with the lowest interference. One problem with low-wing twins is that propeller/ground
   clearance can dictate the nacelle location.
10. Several twins have the outboard aileron station inboard of the wing tip. This happens when
    span is added to a growth version. The tooling expense of extending the ailerons outboard
    is then often not worth it, in particular if the airplane does not need the extra lateral
    control power.
11. A twin-boom pusher such as the Cessna 336 (Fig. 3.9c) has one potential disadvantage:
    failure of an aft propeller blade can cause structural failure of one tailboom.
12. The gull wing of the Piaggio P166-DL3 (Fig. 3.7b) comes from the amphibious airplane this
    design was derived from. Gull wings are often used on amphibious airplanes to keep the
    propeller out of the hull spray. An advantage is the low-interference intersection with the
    fuselage. A disadvantage is the structural discontinuity, which adds wing weight.
13. Many twins have baggage space in the rear of the nacelles.

### §3.1.4 Agricultural airplanes [pp. 42-43]

Figures 3.10-3.12 show twelve recently built agricultural airplanes. Observations [pp. 42-43]:

1. Nine are low-wing and three are bi-plane configurations. All biplanes are externally
   braced, even the jet-powered airplane of Fig. 3.11c. Of the monoplanes, four have
   cantilever wings; the others also have external wing bracing. Most of these ag-airplanes
   also have external bracing of the horizontal tail. The reason for all of this is to keep
   structural weight as low as possible.
2. Eleven are tractor-propeller driven, one is jet driven. Of the propeller configurations,
   only two have turbopropeller installations. The author predicts the turboprop is the future
   trend, because of its greater inherent reliability compared to the piston engine. High
   acquisition cost has been responsible for its slow market penetration.
3. All configurations, except the jet, suffer from a classical ag-airplane problem: the
   propeller slipstream and the wing tip vortices swirl the material that the spray bar
   deposits. The three-views do not show the spraybar systems. On all the airplanes shown, the
   spraybar is mounted close to the wing trailing edge. Part IV (Ref. 3) gives information on
   the location, sizing, and design of spraybar systems.
4. All except the jet have raised cockpits, for good visibility. Pilot visibility is absolutely
   essential in an agricultural airplane, because these airplanes maneuver in and out of very
   difficult spots with obstacles everywhere. The HAL HA-31 of Fig. 3.10b is an extreme
   variation on the visibility theme. The price of so-called 360-degree visibility is high drag.
5. All are designed for crash survivability. This is not directly obvious from the
   configurations. But all have some support structure above the pilot head, in case of an
   inverted crash. The jet of Fig. 3.11c seems to have a pilot-survivability problem in a
   head-on collision with an obstacle.
6. All except two are taildraggers. Most ag operators feel that, because they operate from very
   rough fields, the weight penalty of a nose gear is not worth the better ground handling.
7. The hoppers in most ag-planes are mounted ahead of the pilot. The consensus is that this
   improves crash survivability. But if the hopper leaks, the pilot can be exposed to
   chemicals. On the IA 53 (Fig. 3.11a) and the HAL HA-31 (Fig. 3.10b) the hoppers are below
   the pilot. On the WSK-Mielec M-15 (Fig. 3.11c) the hoppers are in the fuselage behind the
   pilot and in the wing-strut containers. On the Antonov An-2M (Fig. 3.10a, not specifically
   designed for ag duties) the hoppers are in the cabin behind the cockpit.
   A method used on several ag airplanes to remove chemicals from the cockpit is to apply a
   small amount of pressurization with escape vents in suitable places. Such a directed leakage
   path keeps most of the undesirable compounds away from the operator.
8. Bird-proof windshields, wire cutters, and wire deflectors are necessary on all ag-planes.
   Birdproof windshields cause a weight penalty, in particular when the windshield is large.
   Wire cutters are usually mounted ahead of the windscreen. Wire deflectors usually run from
   the top of the canopy or cockpit roof to the top of the vertical tail.
9. Most ag-planes have non-retractable landing gears. Three arguments against a retractable
   gear on an ag-airplane:
   - a) complexity and maintenance
   - b) pilots tend to forget to lower the landing gear
   - c) the drag advantage is small on an airplane that has very large drag increments from
     bracing, spray-bar, and raised windshield.

### §3.1.5 Business jets [p. 47]

Figures 3.13-3.15 show twelve business jets. Observations [p. 47]:

1. Ten of the twelve are twins, one is a tri-jet (Fig. 3.13d), and one has four jet engines
   (Fig. 3.13a).
2. All have the engines in nacelles on the rear fuselage. The French pioneered this
   configuration on the Sud Caravelle jet transport. The middle engine of the Falcon 50 is an
   exception: it is installed like the center engine of the Boeing 727.
3. The early business jets had severe fuel-volume problems. Tiptanks and slipper tanks show
   this (Figs. 3.13a, 3.13b, 3.14b, and 3.15c). The cause was the very high specific fuel
   consumption of the early jet engines.
4. Business jets were among the first airplane types to use winglets for lower induced drag.
   Examples: Figs. 3.13c and 3.14c. [See the misprint note below.]
5. Several recent business jets use a supercritical wing, which improves dragrise behavior.
   Examples: Figs. 3.13c, 3.14a, 3.15a, and 3.15d.
6. All business jets retract the landing gear into the wing or into the wing/fuselage
   intersection.
7. All business jets have a low wing, except the Westwind (Fig. 3.15c). The Westwind was
   developed from the propeller driven Aero Commander, which had a mid-wing configuration.
8. Only four of the business jets have T-tails. The rest have cruciform or low tails.
9. All landing gears are tricycle. Single and double wheels are both used.
10. Three of the business jets have essentially no sweep angle. This implies a thin wing at
    high speed (Learjet, Fig. 3.13b) or a thick wing at somewhat lower speed (Citation, Fig.
    3.14c).

### §3.1.6 Regional turbopropeller driven airplanes [p. 51]

Figures 3.16-3.18 show twelve regional turboprop configurations. Observations [p. 51]:

1. Five are high-wing and seven are low-wing configurations.
2. Only one (the Shorts 330, Fig. 3.18d) has external bracing. All the others are cantilever.
   For cruise-type vehicles the drag penalty of external bracing is usually not acceptable,
   despite the weight advantage. Several of these configurations have very large wing aspect
   ratios. **`A` = 12.7 for the BAe 748 (Fig. 3.18c) is the highest.**
3. Four have T-tail empennages, one has a twin vertical tail empennage, and the others have
   conventional horizontal and vertical tails. The position of the horizontal tail relative to
   the propeller slipstream matters for handling qualities and for tail fatigue. The comments
   made for the twins apply here as well. The Beech 1900 has an extra horizontal stabilizing
   surface and taillets.
4. All have the nacelles installed in the wings, and all are tractor configurations.
   Wing-nacelle integration matters for interference drag and for induced drag. Propeller
   distance to the ground, and therefore landing gear length, also decide how a nacelle is
   integrated into a wing. Large differences between the twelve designs show that nacelle
   integration continues to be a large design problem.
5. Most retract the landing gear into a nacelle. Two of the high-wing airplanes use a blister
   fairing on the fuselage for gear retraction. The Jetstream retracts the gear into the wing
   itself. Blister fairings tend to be very draggy, but they give short gear legs and therefore
   save weight. Retracting a gear into the wing is potentially a bad idea: major cut-outs in
   the primary structure usually result, and the wing structure becomes much heavier.

### §3.1.7 Jet transports [p. 55]

Figures 3.19-3.21 show twelve jet transport configurations. Observations [p. 55]:

1. All jet transports have a low wing, except the BAe 146 (Fig. 3.21d).
2. All have the engines in nacelles on the rear fuselage or under the wing. The Tristar (Fig.
   3.20a) has its center engine buried in the fuselage with an S-duct inlet. Boeing pioneered
   jet engines podded below the wing on the B47 bomber, and before that Arado did so in Germany
   during WWII. The French pioneered fuselage-mounted engine nacelles on the Sud Caravelle.
   Figures 3.19-3.21 do not show jet engines buried in the wing. The British pioneered that
   arrangement and used it on many jet bombers and transports: DH Comet, HP Victor, AVRO Vulcan
   (Fig. 1.1), and Vickers Valiant. The BAe Nimrod (Fig. 3.28b), derived from the Comet, also
   uses it.
3. All jet transports retract the landing gear into the wing/fuselage intersection, except the
   BAe 146. The fuselage-mounted gear of the BAe 146 is aerodynamically very clean. But it
   limits lateral stability on the ground, because the distance between the main gears is small.
4. Many jet transports follow the Boeing wing/fuselage arrangement with a wing glove and a wing
   yehudi. The glove/yehudi arrangement (pioneered on the 707) gives favorable drag, a thick
   wing root, and a way to retract the landing gear without interference with the inboard flaps
   or the rear spars. It also gives very good lateral stability on the ground.
5. Most jet transports use a tricycle landing gear. The Boeing 747 uses four main gear struts.
   The DC 10-30 uses three main gear struts.
6. Three jet transports use T-tails. The remainder use low horizontal tails.
7. For in-flight speedbrakes, ten of the jet transports use wing-mounted spoilers. The Fokker
   F-28 and the BAe 146 (Figs. 3.21c and d) use rear-fuselage-mounted clamshell doors.

### §3.1.8 Military trainers [p. 59]

Figure 3.22 shows four propeller driven military trainers. Figures 3.23 and 3.24 show eight jet
driven military trainers. Observations [p. 59]:

1. The propeller driven trainers are used mostly for initial or basic training. The jet driven
   trainers are used for advanced training.
2. All can be equipped with gun and bomb racks.
3. The RFB Fantrainer (Fig. 3.22d) uses a ducted fan with 5 blades. The fan shaft and duct
   structure are an integral part of the fuselage. The forward sweep angle on the wing was
   selected for weight and balance, not for aerodynamic reasons. The Fantrainer is the only
   pusher; the others are tractors.
4. Note the large use of strakes and fins on most trainers. These were probably added during
   flight test as aerodynamic afterthoughts, to correct stall and/or spin problems.
5. Two of the eight jet trainers have high wings; the rest have low wings.
6. All jet trainers shown use engines buried in the fuselage. A major drag problem with all
   these configurations is to fair the relatively large inlets into the wing root or into the
   fuselage with minimum flow distortion. With the single-engine configurations, another
   problem is the bifurcated S-ducts that lead to the engine. These ducts are very critical to
   successful engine operation and need much detail design attention early.
7. The inboard part of the engine inlets is a good distance away from the fuselage. This
   prevents tired boundary layers from entering the inlet duct.
8. All trainers use the tricycle landing gear layout.
9. Tail configurations range from one T-tail to several cruciform and low tail layouts. The
   T-46A (Fig. 3.23b) uses a twin vertical tail, which is unusual for this type of airplane.

### §3.1.9 Fighters [pp. 63-64]

Figures 3.25-3.27 show twelve fighter configurations. Observations [pp. 63-64]:

1. All fighters are jet driven, except the Pucara (printed as Fig. 3.26c; the Pucara is
   actually Fig. 3.26d). Unless there is a requirement for high dash speeds, the turboprop
   gives a cost advantage and a take-off performance advantage over jets.
2. Six of the fighters are singles and six are twins.
3. All fighters shown have a tricycle landing gear. Note the tandem wheel arrangement on the
   Viggen (Fig. 3.27d). That arrangement lets the gear retract into the thin wing.
4. Most of the fighters shown use only one wheel per main strut. But four of them have two
   nosewheel tires.
5. Two of the fighters shown use a variable sweep wing. This is a very expensive feature in
   weight and in cost. It is justified only when supersonic range, subsonic range, and low
   speed performance are all critical to the mission.
6. Note the preference for conventional configurations. The Mirage IIIE (Fig. 3.26c) uses a
   delta wing. The delta is very efficient if the dominant mission requirement is rapid
   acceleration to supersonic attack or cruise. The blended wing-body of the GD F-16XL (printed
   as Fig. 3.25c; the F-16XL is actually Fig. 3.25d) is a modern variation on the delta theme.
7. SAAB (Sweden) pioneered the coupled canard/delta planform of the Viggen (printed as Fig.
   3.27c). With the canard correctly placed above and forward of the wing, you get very
   favorable interference between the canard lifting vortex and the flow over the delta wing
   behind it.
8. A major design problem with a high speed, highly maneuverable fighter is enough directional
   stability at high angle of attack. Vortices shed from the forebody tend to interfere with
   the vertical tail. This causes some designers to use two large vertical tails. Figures 3.25a
   and 3.27b are typical examples.

### §3.1.10 Military patrol, bomb and transport airplanes [p. 67]

Figures 3.28-3.30 show twelve configurations. Observations [p. 67]:

1. The B52H (Fig. 3.28a) is a long range bomber designed around a long bomb bay. This forced
   the high wing layout and the tandem landing gear. Because of the tandem gear, rotation
   during take-off is not possible: the airplane flies off. This gives longer take-off
   distances. It also forces a rather large wing incidence angle, so the airplane cruises nose
   down, which is not the best attitude for drag. The point: a seemingly innocent and logical
   mission requirement such as a long bomb bay can dominate the configuration design.
2. The transports of Figs. 3.28c, 3.28d, 3.29b, 3.29d, and 3.30a all have high wing layouts
   with body-mounted landing gears. The requirement to load and unload heavy equipment without
   external help forces this feature.
3. The tanker/transports of Figs. 3.29b and 3.29d, and the patrol airplane of Fig. 3.28b, were
   developed from civil transports. That is the main reason for their low wing configurations.
   [See the misprint note below.]
4. The configurations are conventional.
5. Note the large search radars in Figs. 3.30c and 3.30d. These radars are designed for look-up
   as well as look-down capability. The fuselage and the empennage of both airplanes hinder the
   look-down capability to some degree. The empennage of the Hawkeye is made of composites for
   that reason.
6. All jets in this category have significant wing sweep. The turboprops do not. The cause is
   the difference in design cruise speed.
7. Note the four vertical tails on the Hawkeye. This is a carrier-based airplane, with
   restrictions on length, width, and height. This leads to short-coupled configurations. When
   a large radome is added, directional stability decreases, so more vertical tail area is
   necessary. Because height is also restricted, one solution is to add more vertical tails.

### §3.1.11 Flying boats, amphibious and float airplanes [p. 71]

Figures 3.31-3.33 show twelve configurations. Observations [p. 71]:

1. The requirement for a large, hydrodynamically shaped hull dominates these airplanes. This
   gives much larger wetted area and profile drag than a land-based airplane. But it gives a
   unique capability: landing and take-off from water.
2. The airplanes of Figs. 3.31 through 3.32c are modern-era types. The others date from just
   before and during WWII. Note the preference for conventional configurations.
3. A major design problem with water-based airplanes is to keep the hull water spray away from
   engine components: salt water does not agree with most metals.
4. A consequence of item 3 is that the thrust lines in some flying boats are rather high above
   the c.g. This has large consequences for the flying characteristics from power-on to
   power-off.
5. Turboprops are used on most modern flying boats.
6. The Martin Seamaster (Fig. 3.32b) was an attempt to develop a very high speed flying boat
   for the US Navy. Ref. 22 in Section 14.2 gives the reasons for its failure.
7. The huge, ten-engine, turboprop Princess (Fig. 3.32c) was an attempt just after WWII to
   capture the transatlantic passenger market. It failed because the passenger jets (707, DC8,
   Comet) arrived.
8. Most of the amphibians have a tricycle landing gear. The Beriev M12 (Fig. 3.31d) is an
   interesting exception.
9. Landing gear wells in water-based airplanes must be sealed to keep water out of the hull.
   The hulls must have several sealed, watertight compartments. This prevents damage in a small
   area from sinking the whole machine.
